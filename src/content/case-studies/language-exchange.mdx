<CaseSection title="From open-ended browsing to intentional exchanges">
  HablaBA is an invite-only language-exchange product for people in Buenos Aires who want smaller, more intentional ways to practice in person.

I researched, designed, and built HablaBA as a working web product. It covers invitation, profile setup, exchange discovery, session creation and joining, private chat, and notifications.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="The meetup needed a smaller unit of trust">
  Buenos Aires already had language-exchange apps and large events. The gap was a practical way to find a small group, agree on the language and place, and make the session happen.

The first research round was a three-person, 90-minute group interview. I followed it with an 11-response survey and six usability sessions. Across those stages, the useful product shape became clearer: organize around a specific exchange, keep groups small, make neighborhood and timing visible, and give participants one focused place to coordinate.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="Research made the exchange the product">
  <DecisionBlock title="Make the exchange the main object, not the member profile.">
    The prototype started from a simple question: what if people browsed concrete practice opportunities instead of browsing people?

    Each exchange sets the languages, neighborhood, start and end time, one-to-one or group format, participant limit, and description. That gives learners enough context to decide whether a session fits before they join.

  </DecisionBlock>

  <DecisionBlock title="Use real identity where people are planning an in-person meeting.">
    An early concept used generated avatars to reduce the dating-app feel. Usability feedback exposed the cost: people wanted to see who they might meet in a one-to-one or small-group setting.

    The product moved to real profile photos and paired that choice with invite-only registration, verified email, profile completion, and optional two-factor authentication.

  </DecisionBlock>
</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="The wireflow made the exchange loop visible" media wideMedia>

<CaseSection title="People browse exchanges, not profiles">
  The Explore view keeps the decision tied to the session. People can compare the format, languages,
  neighborhood, timing, host, and available places without entering a profile feed.
</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="Coordination stays attached to the exchange">
  <DecisionBlock title="Keep coordination inside the exchange.">
    Joining creates access to that exchange's private chat. Participants can discuss topics, timing, and the meetup without starting a separate social thread or losing the original session details.

    Live messages use private WebSocket channels. In-app and browser notifications cover new participants, chat activity, and canceled sessions, while an activity heartbeat avoids notifying someone who already has the chat open.

  </DecisionBlock>

  <DecisionBlock title="Model the session as a lifecycle, not a static event card.">
    The exchange data model represents active, canceled, and completed states alongside join and leave records.

    Those states matter because the product is trying to help a group complete a real-world plan, not collect passive interest around an event listing.

  </DecisionBlock>
</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="Private chat turns interest into a plan" media>
  Each exchange has one chat for turning interest into a concrete plan. The screenshot uses seeded demo accounts and synthetic conversations from the repository's development data.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="The architecture followed the product loop">
  <DecisionBlock title="Consolidate the maintained product into one web stack.">
    The implementation began as a Rails 8 API with a separate React Native/Expo client. I later built a Flutter client and migrated mobile notifications from Expo Push to Firebase Cloud Messaging. A separate React web client followed. Both mobile clients lived outside this repository and are not part of the maintained codebase.

    I eventually consolidated the maintained product into one Laravel 12, Inertia 2, and Vue 3 web application. That kept the product rules, typed routes, Vue pages, real-time events, queues, and deployment configuration together.

    PostgreSQL stores product state, Redis supports cache and real-time infrastructure, and the production setup separates the app, queue worker, WebSocket server, proxy, and data services in containers.

  </DecisionBlock>

  <DecisionBlock title="Build the trust and operating surfaces with the core experience.">
    The product includes admin-issued invitation links, invitation revocation, a throttled interest form, email verification, password recovery, two-factor authentication, profile deletion, notifications, and light and dark themes.

    Together, they moved the product beyond a clickable prototype and into the work required to test a real community product.

  </DecisionBlock>
</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="What the product made testable">
  The most important decisions came from testing the product model, not adding features. Research moved the experience away from profile browsing. Usability sessions changed the identity approach. Building the product showed that chat, notifications, invitations, and session states were all part of the same coordination problem.

The architecture also changed as the product became clearer. That history is useful evidence: I preserved the product loop while replacing the implementation underneath it, then left a public repository showing the final system and the decisions that shaped it.

</CaseSection>
